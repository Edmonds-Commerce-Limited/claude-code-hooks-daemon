### `get_claude_md()` is ALREADY abstract

`core/handler.py` declares it `@abstractmethod`, alongside `matches`,
`handle` and `get_acceptance_tests`. The probe confirms it empirically: all
107 classes define it (`overrides=True` for every single one).

This corrects Task 3.1's stated rationale. A new handler CANNOT silently
escape *the method* — the ABC makes that impossible. What it can escape is
*triage*: `return None` satisfies the compiler in five seconds and records
nothing about whether the question was considered.

So the gate is still worth building, but its precise job is narrower and
clearer than the plan states: **turn an unexplained `return None` into an
explained one**. The 52 Nones are not 52 omissions — they are 52 decisions
with no recorded reasoning, which is why a release-time human had to
re-derive them by hand.

## Post-completion correction — the gate could not catch its own worst entry

Filed after the plan was marked Complete, because the defect is in what this
plan shipped and the fix belongs with it.

### What was wrong

`ValidateEslintOnWriteHandler` was recorded EXEMPT with the reason "the
ESLint-specific case of `lint_on_edit`, whose resident section already states
that writes are linted and a failure denies".

That reason was wrong twice, and the second way is the dangerous one:

1. `lint_on_edit`'s section lists nine languages and **TypeScript is not among
   them**. A `.ts` author reads that list, does not find their language, and
   concludes they are unchecked — while this handler denies their write.
2. The two handlers **degrade in opposite directions**. `lint_on_edit` ALLOWs
   when its linter is missing or times out, and its section says so in as many
   words. This one DENIES on an ESLint timeout AND on any failure to run ESLint
   at all. So the covering section did not merely omit TypeScript — it stated a
   graceful-degradation guarantee that is FALSE for the case it claimed to
   cover. Guidance that misleads is worse than absent guidance.

### Why the suite as shipped could not find it

Both handlers agreed with their table rows. `ValidateEslintOnWriteHandler`
returned `None` and was listed exempt; `LintOnEditHandler` returned content and
was listed covered. Every consistency check passed. The suite verified that
each verdict MATCHED THE CODE, and had no way to ask whether a verdict was
RIGHT.

Re-reading the reasons found nothing — I wrote them, so I re-read my own
argument and agreed with it. Asking mechanically **"which EXEMPT handlers
contain a `Decision.DENY` path?"** found it on the first try, from one query
over 50 entries.

That is the whole DBF argument in miniature: the review missed what the guard
caught, and the guard only existed because the question was made mechanical.

### The fix, both halves

- `ValidateEslintOnWriteHandler` now has its own section, leading on the
  differentiator rather than repeating the sibling: stricter than
  `lint_on_edit`, denies on timeout, and enforcement is gated on `llm:` scripts
  in `package.json` so silence is not proof of a clean file.
- `lint_on_edit`'s section now scopes its leniency claim explicitly and names
  the sibling, so the "a missing linter never blocks" rule cannot be carried
  across to TypeScript.
- **New guard**: `TestAnExemptHandlerCannotQuietlyDeny`. No exempt handler may
  contain a DENY path unless listed in `_EXEMPT_DESPITE_DENYING` with the
  covering section named. That list ships EMPTY — every deny-capable handler
  now earns its own section — so any future entry is a deliberate argument
  rather than a default.

### The other claim shapes, checked properly

Having found one bad reason I did not assume the rest were sound. The four
"another resident section already carries it" claims were each verified by
reading the referenced section's text for the specific content:
`plan_qa_commit_gate` does carry the `git mv`/README/statistics steps;
`plan_number_helper` does name `mkplan.bash` and the counter; both nitpick
handlers' Stop twins do carry the phrase families. All four hold.
